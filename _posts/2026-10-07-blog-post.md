We give a short proof that \\(\pi\\) is irrational, communicated to me by Vesselin Dimitrov.

> <span class="statement-label" id="thm-pi" data-env="Theorem">Theorem.</span>
The constant \\(\pi\\) is irrational.

One way to show that a constant \\(\gamma\\) is irrational is to exhibit many good rational approximations. This amounts to finding integer linear polynomials \\(aX+b\\) for which \\(a\gamma+b\\) is small. We will proceed similarly by exhibiting many integer polynomials \\(p(X)\\) for which \\(p(\pi)\\) is small. These polynomials will be found using the coefficients of the power series of
\\[f(X):=\cos\big(2\pi\sqrt{1+4X}\big).\\]
(The \\(2\pi\\) and \\(1+4X\\) provide a convenient normalization; they are not essential.) The theorem will follow by understanding the coefficients of the Taylor expansion \\(\sum_na_nX^n\\) of \\(f(X)\\) around \\(X=0\\). Let's collect the needed facts now, in order of difficulty: <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-nonzero">Lemma</a> shows there are infinitely many nonzero coefficients, <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-bound">Lemma</a> shows that the coefficients are small, and <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-integrality">Lemma</a> gives an integrality property involving \\(\pi\\).

> <span class="statement-label" id="lem-nonzero" data-env="Lemma">Lemma.</span>
The coefficient \\(a\_n\\) is nonzero for infinitely many nonnegative integers \\(n\\).

*Proof.*
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that \\(a\_n\ne0\\) for only finitely many \\(n\\). This means that all sufficiently high derivatives of \\(f(X)\\) vanish at \\(X=0\\).
<!-- Then there is a polynomial \\(T(X)\\) (given by the Taylor expansion) for which all derivatives of \\(f(X)-T(X)\\) vanish at \\(X=0\\). -->
However, \\(\cos(4\pi X)=f\big(X^2-1/4\big)\\), so all sufficiently high derivatives of \\(\cos(4\pi X)\\) must vanish at \\(X=1/2\\). But the sequence of derivatives of \\(\cos(X)\\) is \\(4\\)-periodic and not constantly zero at \\(X=2\pi\\), so we have a contradiction. \\(\blacksquare\\)

> <span class="statement-label" id="lem-bound" data-env="Lemma">Lemma.</span>
There is an absolute constant \\(c>0\\) such that for all sufficiently large integers \\(n\\),
\\[\left\|a\_n\right\|<\frac{c^n}{(2n)!}.\\]

*Proof.*
This is a direct calculation. All series we consider will converge absolutely for \\(X\in[-1/8,1/8]\\) because factorials dominate exponentials. This justifies our rearrangements; we will not comment on this again. Using the Taylor expansion of \\(\cos\\) and the Binomial theorem, we have
\\[\begin{aligned}f(X) &= \sum\_{m=0}^\infty(-1)^m\frac{(2\pi)^{2m}(1+4X)^m}{(2m)!} \\\\{}
&= \sum\_{n=0}^\infty\left(\sum\_{m=n}^\infty(-1)^m\frac{(2\pi)^{2m}\cdot\binom{m}{n}4^n}{(2m)!}\right)X^n.\end{aligned}\\]
It remains to estimate this "series expansion" of \\(a\_n\\). By the triangle inequality and the esimate \\(\binom{m}{n}\le2^m\\),
\\[\left\|a\_n\right\|\le\frac{\left(32\pi^2\right)^n}{(2n)!}\sum\_{m=n}^\infty\frac{\left(8\pi^2\right)^{m-n}}{(2m)!/(2n)!}\\]
The series converges and is decreasing in \\(n\\), so any \\(c>32\pi^2\\) will work. \\(\blacksquare\\)

> <span class="statement-label" id="lem-integrality" data-env="Lemma">Lemma.</span>
For each nonnegative integer \\(n\\), we have \\(n!a\_n\in\operatorname{span}\_{\mathbb Z}\\{1,\pi,\ldots,\pi^n\\}\\).

*Proof.*
The main claim is that
\\[(1+4X)f\'\'(X)+2f'(X)+16\pi^2f(X)\stackrel?=0.\\]
<!-- As an aside, we note that this property allows one to study \\(f\\) using the theory of arithmetic holonomy. -->
Verifying this is a direct calculation. Here is a convenient execution: by taking real parts, it is enough to show the same claim for \\(g(X):=\exp(2\pi i\sqrt{1+4X})\\). But
\\[\sqrt{1+4X}\cdot g'(X)=4\pi ig(X).\\]
Taking another derivative and rearranging proves the claim.

We now use the claim to complete the proof. For brevity, define \\(b\_n:=n!a\_n\\) so that \\(f(X)=\sum_nb\_n\frac{X^n}{n!}\\). For any nonnegative integer \\(n\\), taking the \\(X^n\\)-coefficient in the main claim shows that
\\[(b\_{n+2}+4nb\_{n+1})+2b\_{n+1}+16\pi^2b\_n=0.\\]
The lemma now follows by induction upon computing the base cases \\(b\_0=f(0)=1\\) and \\(b\_1=f'(0)=0\\). \\(\blacksquare\\)

> <span class="statement-label" id="rem-alt-integral" data-env="Remark">Remark.</span>
Another way to prove <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-integrality">Lemma</a> is to write
\\[f(X)=\cos\big(2\pi\big(\sqrt{1+4X}-1\big)\big)\\]
and plug the Taylor expansion of \\(\sqrt{1+4X}-1\\) into the Taylor expansion of \\(\cos\\).

Thus, the coefficients \\(n!a\_n\\) provide many integer polynomials which are small at \\(\pi\\). This is enough to deduce irrationality.

*Proof of <a class="statement-ref" href="#thm-pi">Theorem</a>.*
Suppose for the sake of contradiction that \\(\pi\\) is rational. We will use <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-bound">Lemma</a> and <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-integrality">Lemma</a> to show that \\(a\_n=0\\) for sufficiently large \\(n\\), which contradicts <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-nonzero">Lemma</a>.
<!-- This claim quickly produces contradiction: for example, because \\(\cos(4\pi X)=f\big((X-1/4)^2\big)\\), the claim would imply that \\(\cos(4\pi X)\\) has only finitely many nonzero derivatives at \\(X=1/4\\), which is false. -->
<!-- \\(f(X)\\) is real analytic on \\((-1/4,\infty)\\), so \\(f(X)\\) must then be a polynomial on \\((-1/4,\infty)\\), which is false because \\(f(X)\\) is non-constant and has infinitely many roots. -->

Write \\(\pi=p/q\\) where \\(p\\) and \\(q\\) are positive integers. On one hand, by <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-integrality">Lemma</a>, \\(q^nn!a\_n\in\mathbb Z\\) for all nonnegative integers \\(n\\). Thus, it is enough to show that \\(\left\|q^nn!a\_n\right\|<1\\) for sufficiently large \\(n\\). On the other hand, by <a class="statement-ref" href="#lem-bound">Lemma</a>, there is an absolute constant \\(c>0\\) such that
\\[\left|q^nn!a\_n\right\|<q^nn!\cdot\frac{c^n}{(2n)!}\\]
for all sufficiently large integers \\(n\\). We conclude because \\((cq)^nn!/(2n)!\to0\\) as \\(n\to\infty\\). \\(\blacksquare\\)
